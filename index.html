Folly Island · Charleston County · South Carolina

Folly Beach, South Carolina:
The Edge of America

Six miles of open Atlantic shoreline, a 1,049-foot fishing pier, the best surf on the South Carolina coast and a lighthouse standing alone in the sea, all twenty minutes from downtown Charleston.

About Folly Beach

Folly Beach is a small barrier island city in Charleston County, roughly 12 miles south of downtown Charleston at the end of Folly Road (SC Highway 171). The island is about six miles long and rarely more than half a mile wide, with the Atlantic on one side and the Folly River, tidal creeks and spartina marsh on the other. Locals call it "the Edge of America," and the nickname fits: this is the closest true beach town to the historic peninsula, and it has never lost its laid-back, slightly offbeat personality.

Around two thousand people live here year-round (the U.S. Census Bureau counted 2,078 residents in 2020), but on a summer Saturday the population swells many times over with day-trippers, surfers, families and weekend renters. Unlike the gated resort islands to the south, Folly is a working, walkable town with a real main street, independent restaurants, surf shops, bars with live music and a public pier. It is governed by the City of Folly Beach, and its two big public parks are run by Charleston County Parks.

The name itself is old. "Folly" is an archaic English word for a dense thicket of trees or foliage, and early mariners described the island that way long before anyone built a cottage on it. Today the island is a mix of raised beach houses, colorful rentals, a handful of hotels and inns, and protected land at both ends where the island still looks much like it did a century ago.

What Folly Beach Is Famous For

Folly Beach is famous for its surf, its pier, the Morris Island Lighthouse, and an eclectic, unpretentious beach-town culture that sets it apart from the rest of the Charleston coast. It is widely considered the surfing capital of South Carolina, with the Washout on the east end producing the most consistent waves in the state. The rebuilt Edwin S. Taylor Folly Beach Fishing Pier is one of the longest on the East Coast, and the lonely Morris Island Lighthouse, now standing in open water off the island's northern tip, is among the most photographed landmarks in the Lowcountry.

The island also carries real cultural history. In the summer of 1934, composer George Gershwin rented a cottage on Folly while writing the score for Porgy and Bess, soaking up the Gullah music and speech of nearby James Island to shape the opera. During the Civil War, Union troops occupied the island and used it as a staging ground for the assault on Battery Wagner on Morris Island, the battle made famous by the 54th Massachusetts and the film Glory. And for generations, Folly has been the people's beach for Charleston: the place locals have gone after work, on the Fourth of July and on New Year's Day for the polar plunge.

Add in Center Street's mix of surf shops, taco joints and dive bars, a reputation for live music, and a slightly rebellious spirit (the island has its own festivals, its own quirks and plenty of personality), and you have a beach that people describe as "the least Charleston part of Charleston." That contrast is a big part of the appeal.

Folly Beach Is Worth the Trip for Most Visitors

Yes, Folly Beach is worth going to, especially if you want a casual, walkable beach town with character rather than a polished resort. It is the easiest real beach to reach from downtown Charleston, it has public parking and public beach access along the whole island, and it packs surfing, fishing, a lighthouse, wildlife, good food and nightlife into a few square miles.

Folly is ideal for day-trippers staying in Charleston, surfers and surf students, anglers, groups of friends, bachelorette parties, couples who want a sunset dinner on the water, and families who prefer a lively atmosphere. You can walk from the pier to a dozen restaurants and bars, rent a board or a bike, and be back on the peninsula for dinner the same evening.

It may be less ideal if you want a quiet, manicured resort with a golf course, or if you hate traffic. Folly Road is the only road on and off the island, so summer weekend afternoons can mean slow going, and parking near the center fills early. The fix is simple: arrive before 10 a.m., stay through sunset, or visit on a weekday. Spring and fall are the sweet spot, with warm water, fewer crowds and lower rates. If you want a calmer Charleston beach experience, the island's west end around Folly Beach County Park feels noticeably more relaxed than the busy center.

History of Folly Island

Long before European settlement, the Lowcountry's barrier islands were fishing and shellfish grounds for the Native peoples of the coast, including the Bohicket and Stono. Under English colonial rule the island changed hands among planters and investors, but its shifting sand and lack of fresh water kept it largely wild for two centuries. Ships wrecked on its shoals often enough that the waters off Folly and Morris Island became notorious among mariners approaching Charleston Harbor.

Civil War Occupation

In 1863, Union forces moved onto Folly Island and fortified it as a launching point for the campaign against Confederate positions on Morris Island, which guarded the entrance to Charleston Harbor and Fort Sumter. Thousands of soldiers camped among the dunes, and in July 1863 regiments including the 54th Massachusetts crossed Lighthouse Inlet to attack Battery Wagner. Relics, camp sites and even remains of soldiers have surfaced on the island over the years, and the American Battlefield Trust and South Carolina Historical Society are good starting points for anyone who wants to dig into that chapter.

From Ferry Island to Beach Town

Folly became reachable by car in the early 1920s when a road and bridge connected it to James Island, and almost immediately it turned into Charleston's playground. A wooden pier made of palmetto logs went up in 1931, followed by a pavilion, boardwalk, dance halls and amusement rides that drew big bands and crowds through the mid-century era. Fire claimed the pier and pavilion more than once, most notably in 1957 and again in January 1977, and the island went nearly two decades without a pier before Charleston County Parks opened a new one in 1995.

The Gershwin Summer

George Gershwin spent the summer of 1934 in a small frame cottage on Folly, working with Charleston author DuBose Heyward on Porgy and Bess. Gershwin wrote home about the heat, the bugs and the beauty of the place, and he spent time at Gullah churches on nearby James Island absorbing the rhythms that ended up in the score. The Charleston Museum and Historic Charleston Foundation both cover the city's ties to the opera.

Storms, Erosion and Renewal

Hurricane Hugo struck in 1989 and reshaped the island, flattening homes and washing over the east end so badly that a stretch of road and houses simply disappeared, creating the surf spot now known as the Washout. Since then the beach has been rebuilt through periodic renourishment projects led by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Charleston District. The pier was replaced again beginning in 2020, and the new concrete-piling structure opened on December 26, 2022, with a projected lifespan of 65-plus years.

Things To Do on Folly Beach

The beach itself is the main event, but Folly rewards people who explore beyond their towel. Here is the full rundown of what to see and do on and around the island.

Walk or Fish the Folly Beach Pier

The Edwin S. Taylor Folly Beach Fishing Pier stretches 1,049 feet into the Atlantic at 101 East Arctic Avenue. It is open daily from 8 a.m. to sunset (hours vary by season), and admission to walk it is inexpensive, with paid parking in the pier lot. The Gangplank Gift & Tackle Shop rents rods and sells bait, and anglers pull in whiting, spot, flounder, red drum, Spanish mackerel and the occasional shark. Sunrise from the end of the pier is one of the best free shows in the Lowcountry, and the pier hosts fishing tournaments and summer dance nights.

See the Morris Island Lighthouse

Drive or bike to the very end of East Ashley Avenue and walk the sandy path through the Lighthouse Inlet Heritage Preserve to the beach. From there you will see the Morris Island Lighthouse standing offshore. Completed in 1876 and rising about 161 feet, it once sat well inland, but erosion after jetty construction in Charleston Harbor left it surrounded by water. It is not open to visitors, but the view at sunrise is spectacular. The nonprofit Save The Light raised money to stabilize the tower and shares its history.

Surf the Washout

Folly is the heart of South Carolina surfing. The Washout, along East Ashley Avenue, catches the most swell, while the waters around the pier offer gentler peelers for beginners. Check the forecast on Surfline, and if you have never stood on a board, book a lesson through one of the local schools listed in the Folly Beach surf lessons directory. Board rentals are easy to find on Center Street at shops like McKevlin's Surf Shop, one of the oldest surf shops on the East Coast.

Spend a Day at Folly Beach County Park

On the island's west end, Folly Beach County Park offers lifeguarded swimming in season, restrooms, outdoor showers, picnic areas and chair and umbrella rentals. It is the most family-friendly stretch of sand on the island, and its back side along the Folly River is excellent for birding, with pelicans, oystercatchers, terns and herons working the inlet.

Kayak, Paddleboard and Dolphin Tours

The Folly River and the creeks behind the island are calm and full of wildlife. Guided kayak and paddleboard tours regularly spot bottlenose dolphins, including the Lowcountry's famous strand-feeding dolphins that herd fish onto mud banks. Boat charters run out to Morris Island for shelling and lighthouse views, and inshore fishing guides target redfish and trout in the marsh.

Shop and Stroll Center Street

Center Street is Folly's main drag, running from the bridge to the pier. It is packed with surf shops, beachwear boutiques, ice cream counters, galleries, bars and restaurants, and it is where most visitors end up after the sun goes down. Pick up groceries, beer and beach supplies at Bert's Market, the island's beloved round-the-clock market on East Ashley.

Turtle Season and Wildlife Watching

From May through October, loggerhead sea turtles nest on Folly's beaches. Volunteers mark and monitor nests, and the city enforces lights-out rules on the beachfront after dark so hatchlings can find the ocean. Learn more through SCDNR's sea turtle program and the South Carolina Aquarium, whose Sea Turtle Care Center rehabilitates injured turtles.

Areas of the Island Explained

Folly is small, but each section has a distinct feel. Knowing them helps you pick where to stay and where to park.

Center Street and the Pier District

The busy middle of the island, where Folly Road becomes Center Street and ends at the ocean. This is where you will find the pier, the oceanfront hotel, most restaurants and bars, and the liveliest crowd. Great for groups and anyone who wants to walk everywhere.

East End and the Washout

East Ashley Avenue runs northeast past beach houses to the Washout surf break and finally the Lighthouse Inlet Heritage Preserve. It is quieter than the center, popular with surfers and rental guests, and home to the island's best sunrise views.

West End and the County Park

West Ashley Avenue leads past residential streets to Folly Beach County Park. This end of the island has a calmer, family vacation feel, wide beaches at low tide and sunset views over the Folly River.

The Marsh Side

Streets on the river side of the island look out over tidal creeks and docks rather than the ocean. Rentals here are often more affordable and offer some of the island's best sunsets, with the beach only a few blocks away.

Hotels and Places To Stay

Folly has only a handful of traditional hotels, so book early for summer weekends. Most visitors stay in vacation rentals, which range from tiny beach cottages to large oceanfront homes, and many day-trippers simply base themselves in Charleston or on James Island.

Oceanfront

Tides Folly Beach

The island's only full-service oceanfront hotel, at 1 Center Street right beside the pier. Rooms come with private balconies facing the Atlantic, and there is a heated oceanfront pool, a fitness room and on-site dining at the beachfront bar and restaurant. The best choice if you want to step from your room to the sand and walk to everything.

Adults Only

Water's Edge Inn

A small adults-only inn on the island with water views, aimed at couples who want a quieter, more grown-up stay than the busy hotel scene. Good for anniversaries and weekend getaways, and an easy walk or bike ride to Center Street restaurants.

Marsh & River

Regatta Inn Folly Beach

A boutique inn at 64 West 9th Street Extension on the river side of the island, set among marsh and docks. Expect a more private, residential feel with sunset views over the water, a short drive or bike from the beach and downtown Folly.

Most Popular

Vacation Rentals

Hundreds of beach houses and condos fill the island, many listed through local property managers as well as the big booking platforms. Oceanfront homes on East and West Ashley go fastest. The City of Folly Beach regulates short-term rentals, so book licensed properties and check occupancy and parking rules.

10 Minutes Away

James Island Hotels

Chain and independent hotels along Folly Road and Maybank Highway on James Island sit 10 to 15 minutes from the beach, usually at lower nightly rates. A smart pick for budget travelers or anyone splitting time between Folly and the city.

Historic District

Downtown Charleston

Stay among the historic inns and boutique hotels of the peninsula and drive 20 to 25 minutes to Folly for beach days. This works well for first-time Charleston visitors who want both the city and the shore. See Explore Charleston for lodging listings.

Local Eats on Folly Beach

Folly's food scene is casual, fun and heavy on fresh seafood, tacos and breakfast. Almost everything is independently owned, and flip-flops are acceptable nearly everywhere.

Breakfast

Lost Dog Cafe

The island's breakfast institution on West Huron, famous for hearty plates, breakfast burritos and a dog-friendly patio covered in photos of locals' pups. Expect a line on weekends; it moves.

Seafood & Views

Rita's Seaside Grille

Right at the foot of Center Street across from the beach, Rita's is a go-to for shrimp baskets, fish tacos, burgers and frozen drinks on a big open patio. Lively with families by day and a younger crowd by night.

Tacos

Taco Boy

Colorful folk-art taco spot on Center Street serving street-style tacos, fresh salsas, margaritas and a long list of tequila. One of the best values on the island and great for groups.

On the Pier

Pier 101 Restaurant & Bar

Sits at the base of the Folly Beach Pier with the best ocean view on the island. Order fried shrimp, fish sandwiches or a cold beer and watch surfers and pelicans from the deck.

Island Vibes

Chico Feo

A tiny open-air spot on East Ashley with picnic tables under the trees, rotating island-style plates and some of Folly's best free live music. It captures the island's personality better than almost anywhere.

Oceanfront Bar

Pinky's at Tides

The beachfront restaurant and bar at Tides Folly Beach, steps from the sand and the pier. A relaxed place for lunch, a sunset cocktail or a drink after a day on the beach.

Center Street

Coconut Joe's

A Center Street favorite with a rooftop deck, ocean breezes, seafood baskets, burgers and frozen drinks. Good for families at lunch and groups at happy hour.

Market & Deli

Bert's Market

The island's iconic market keeps late hours and stocks everything from breakfast biscuits and deli sandwiches to sunscreen, beer and boogie boards. Every Folly regular has a Bert's story.

Just Off Island

Bowens Island Restaurant

On the way to Folly off Folly Road, this legendary James Island seafood shack is known for shovel-loads of roasted oysters, fried shrimp and hushpuppies over the marsh. A James Beard America's Classics honoree and a Lowcountry rite of passage.

Pizza, ice cream, coffee and late-night bites are also spread along Center Street and East Ashley. For more Charleston-area food ideas, browse Charleston City Paper and The Post and Courier's food coverage. Restaurant hours change seasonally, so check before you go in the off-season.

Nightlife and Live Music

Folly has the most beach-town nightlife in the Charleston area. Bars along Center Street and East Ashley stay busy after dark, many feature live bands or acoustic sets on weekends, and the scene runs from rooftop decks and oyster bars to sandy-floored dives. It is a favorite stop for bachelorette weekends, birthday trips and anyone who wants a night out without heading downtown.

For a bigger picture of where to go out across the region, including King Street bar crawls, bachelorette party ideas and the broader Charleston party scene, Party Charleston covers the whole map. Folly-specific news, events and dining updates are also posted at Folly Beach Now.

A few practical notes: alcohol is not allowed on the beach itself, so drinking stays inside bars and restaurants or on private property. Rideshare pickups get slow after midnight on summer weekends because of the single road, so plan your ride home early or stay on the island.

Beach Rules and Local Ordinances

Folly's rules are enforced, and tickets are common for visitors who do not know them. These are the big ones, but always confirm current ordinances with the City of Folly Beach before your trip.

Alcohol and glass. Alcohol has been banned on Folly's beaches since 2012, and glass containers are not allowed on the sand. Fireworks. Personal fireworks are prohibited on the island, including on the Fourth of July. Dogs. Leashed dogs are allowed on the beach with seasonal time restrictions during the summer months, and owners must clean up after pets. Parking. Park only in marked spaces with all four tires off the pavement where required, pay at kiosks or by app in paid zones, and never block driveways; towing and fines are frequent. Dunes and turtles. Stay off the dunes and sea oats, fill in holes you dig, and keep beachfront lights off at night during nesting season. Swimming. Rip currents are a real hazard, especially near the pier and at the Washout. Swim near lifeguards at the County Park when possible and heed beach flags.

The Prettiest Beach in Charleston Depends on Your Style

Many visitors and locals name Folly Beach the prettiest beach in Charleston for its sunrise views of the Morris Island Lighthouse and the wild scenery at both ends of the island, but every Charleston beach has its own kind of beauty. For raw natural scenery, Kiawah Island's Beachwalker Park and the boneyard beaches at nearby state parks are hard to beat. For calm water and quiet, Sullivan's Island is a local favorite. For a polished resort look, Isle of Palms delivers wide, groomed sand.

Folly wins on variety: in a single day you can watch the sun rise over the lighthouse, surf at the Washout, walk a thousand feet out over the ocean on the pier, and watch the sun set over the marsh from the west end. The Lighthouse Inlet Heritage Preserve at the north end and the protected County Park at the south end keep both tips of the island scenic and undeveloped.

If you are choosing between Charleston beaches, consider this quick comparison. Folly Beach is closest to downtown, best for surfing, and the liveliest. Sullivan's Island is quiet, residential and loaded with Fort Moultrie history. Isle of Palms is the classic family resort beach with Wild Dunes. Kiawah Island is upscale and natural, with limited public access at Kiawah Beachwalker Park. Many visitors try more than one during a trip.

Flying Into Folly Beach Starts at Charleston International Airport

To reach Folly Beach, SC, fly into Charleston International Airport (CHS). It is the closest commercial airport, about 20 miles from the island, and the drive typically takes 30 to 40 minutes depending on traffic. Rental cars, rideshare and taxis are all available at the terminal.

Charleston International, located in North Charleston, offers nonstop flights to dozens of U.S. cities on major and low-cost carriers. From the airport, take I-526 west toward West Ashley and James Island, then follow the James Island Connector or Folly Road (SC-171) south all the way onto the island. Folly Road becomes Center Street once you cross the bridge.

If fares out of CHS are high, two alternatives are within driving distance. Savannah/Hilton Head International (SAV) is about two hours south, and Myrtle Beach International (MYR) is about two and a half hours north. Columbia Metropolitan (CAE) is roughly two hours inland.

Getting Around the Island

A car is the easiest way to reach Folly, but once you are here, bikes, golf carts and your own two feet cover most of the island. Public transit from downtown is limited, so most visitors drive or use rideshare. In peak season, arrive early for parking, consider biking from your rental, and expect slower traffic on Folly Road on weekend afternoons and holidays. The SC Department of Transportation posts road and bridge updates.

Annual Events and Festivals

Folly's calendar mixes small-town traditions with big beach parties. On New Year's Day, hundreds of people charge into the cold Atlantic for the polar plunge. Spring brings Folly Gras, the island's take on Mardi Gras with music, food and vendors on Center Street. The pier hosts fishing tournaments and summer dance nights, and Fourth of July weekend is the island's busiest of the year, with huge crowds and heavy traffic.

Fall is festival season, with the Sea and Sand Festival and other street events celebrating local music, art and food, plus surf contests whenever good swell lines up. Charleston County Parks also runs seasonal programs at the pier and the County Park. For current dates, check the city's website, Visit Folly, and Charleston County Parks events.

Weather and the Best Time To Visit

Folly has a humid subtropical climate. Summers are hot and sticky, with highs in the upper 80s to low 90s and frequent afternoon thunderstorms, while ocean temperatures climb into the 80s by July. Winters are mild, with highs in the upper 50s and 60s, making the island pleasant for walks, fishing and birding even when it is too cold to swim.

The best time to visit for most people is May through early June or September through October, when the water is warm, the crowds thin out and rates drop. Hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30, with the highest risk from August through October, so watch the National Hurricane Center and the National Weather Service Charleston office before and during any late-summer trip. Tide times matter for beach width, shelling and fishing, and the NOAA Tides & Currents site posts predictions for Folly and nearby stations. Beach water quality advisories are published by South Carolina's state environmental agency.

Living on Folly Beach

Year-round life on Folly is close-knit and outdoorsy. Residents surf before work, walk dogs on the beach at sunrise and know their bartenders by name. Homes range from vintage cottages and mid-century bungalows to new raised construction built to modern flood standards, and oceanfront property commands premium prices. Flood insurance, wind coverage and elevation requirements are a big part of owning here, and the FEMA flood map service is the place to start any property research.

Folly is part of Charleston County, and children attend schools on James Island through the Charleston County School District. Groceries, hardware stores and services are a short drive away on James Island, and downtown jobs are about 20 minutes away. For county records, property data and services, see Charleston County Government. Lifestyle coverage of the Lowcountry, including real estate and recreation, is a regular topic at Simply Lowcountry.

Nearby Attractions Beyond the Island

James Island sits just across the bridge and is home to James Island County Park, with its climbing wall, dog park, splash zone waterpark and the Holiday Festival of Lights in winter. History buffs can visit Fort Lamar Heritage Preserve, site of the 1862 Battle of Secessionville, and the McLeod Plantation Historic Site, which tells the stories of the enslaved people who lived and worked there.

Downtown Charleston is 20 to 25 minutes away, with the historic district, King Street shopping, the Battery, the City Market, and ferries to Fort Sumter National Monument. The South Carolina Aquarium on the harbor is a must with kids, and the Preservation Society of Charleston offers walking tours and history. Kiawah and Seabrook Islands lie about 40 minutes southwest, while Sullivan's Island and Isle of Palms are about 40 minutes northeast via the Ravenel Bridge and Mount Pleasant.

Grab coffee for the drive at one of the city's independent cafes highlighted on City Lights Coffee, and look into Discover South Carolina for statewide trip ideas.
